# Talk: Why Virtual Worlds Need Real Lobbies
Target length: 12 minutes
Audience: game design students

## Opening — the empty world problem
Start with the story of logging into our first playtest and finding nobody
there. The world was beautiful and completely empty, and an empty world
feels broken even when nothing is technically wrong. Key stat: players
decide whether a social world feels alive within the first ninety seconds.
(Keep this tight — don't get drawn into the rendering anecdote.)

## What a lobby actually does
A lobby is not a waiting room, it is the first room of the experience.
Three jobs: it teaches the controls without a tutorial, it proves other
people exist, and it gives friends a place to gather before the activity
starts. Mention the airport analogy — nobody remembers the flight, but
everybody remembers whether the departure hall felt chaotic or calm.

## Lessons from our playtests
Playtest one: players spawned alone and churn went up. Playtest two: we
added a shared campfire space and average session length doubled. The
campfire worked because it gave strangers a reason to stand near each
other. (If the campfire clip is available, describe it here instead of
the metrics — don't do both.)

## Design rules we now follow
Rule one: never spawn a player alone. Rule two: every lobby needs one
shared object people naturally gather around. Rule three: keep the path
from lobby to activity under ten seconds. These rules sound obvious and
every one of them came from a mistake we actually made.

## Closing — build the doorway first
End where we started: the first ninety seconds decide everything. If you
build the doorway first, the world behind it gets a chance to matter.
Invite questions, and point people to the demo table after the session.
(End on the doorway line, not on the thanks slide.)
